# Federal Civil Appeals to the Eighth Circuit

Preserves and perfects a federal civil appeal, where the first deadline (the notice of appeal) is jurisdictional and unforgiving.

## When to use
- A federal district court dismissed the case, granted summary judgment, or entered a final judgment
- Deciding whether an interlocutory order (injunction ruling, qualified-immunity denial) is appealable now
- Planning the record, transcript, briefing, rehearing, or a certiorari petition
- Not for: Missouri state appeals — use `mo-appeals-procedure`; habeas appeals requiring a certificate of appealability — use `federal-habeas-2254`

## Gather first
- The exact docket entry date of the judgment or order, and whether a separate Rule 58 judgment was entered
- Any post-judgment motions filed (type and filing date) and their disposition dates
- Whether the United States, its agency, or officer is a party (changes deadlines)

## Workflow
1. **Appealability.**
   - Final decisions: 28 U.S.C. 1291 — a decision that ends the litigation on the merits leaving nothing but execution.
   - Partial judgments: only with Rule 54(b) certification (express determination of no just reason for delay).
   - Interlocutory appeals as of right: orders granting, refusing, modifying, or dissolving injunctions (28 U.S.C. 1292(a)(1)).
   - Certified questions: district court certifies under 1292(b); petition to the court of appeals within 10 days after entry of the order.
   - Collateral orders (Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541 (1949)), including denials of qualified immunity on legal grounds (Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985)).
2. **Notice of appeal deadline (FRAP 4(a)).** 30 days after entry of the judgment or order; 60 days if the United States, a U.S. agency, or a U.S. officer or employee (in specified capacities) is a party. The statutory civil appeal deadline is jurisdictional and cannot be excused for equitable reasons. Bowles v. Russell, 551 U.S. 205 (2007).
3. **Tolling motions (FRAP 4(a)(4)).** A timely motion under Rule 50(b), 52(b), 54 (fees, only if the court extends time under Rule 58), 59 (alter or amend, or new trial), or 60 (if filed within 28 days after judgment) restarts the appeal clock from the order disposing of the last such motion. Rule 50, 52, and 59 motions are due 28 days after entry of judgment and cannot be extended. A notice filed before disposition ripens upon disposition; to challenge the ruling on the motion itself, file a new or amended notice.
4. **Rescue options.** Motion for extension within 30 days after the original deadline expires, showing excusable neglect or good cause (4(a)(5)). Reopening when a party did not receive Rule 77(d) notice of entry (4(a)(6)): narrow conditions and short windows — read the rule's exact text.
5. **Separate-document rule.** If no separate judgment document is entered when required, judgment is deemed entered 150 days after entry in the civil docket (FRAP 4(a)(7); Rule 58). Do not rely on this — appeal within 30 days of the operative order where possible.
6. **The notice (FRAP 3).** File in the district court (not the Eighth Circuit). Specify the party or parties taking the appeal, designate the judgment or appealable order, and name the court of appeals. Designating the final judgment brings up the interlocutory orders that merged into it. Pay the docketing fee or move for IFP on appeal (FRAP 24; see `federal-ifp-1915`). Incarcerated filers: FRAP 4(c) prison-mailbox rule.
7. **Record and transcript (FRAP 10, 11).** Order needed transcripts (or certify none needed) within 14 days after filing the notice of appeal. The record is the district-court filings, exhibits, and transcripts — no new evidence on appeal.
8. **Stay pending appeal (FRAP 8).** Ordinarily move first in the district court; then in the court of appeals, showing why district-court relief was impracticable or denied.
9. **Briefing (FRAP 28, 30, 31, 32; Eighth Circuit Local Rules).** The clerk issues a briefing schedule — follow it. FRAP 28(a) contents: corporate disclosure (if applicable), tables, jurisdictional statement, statement of issues, statement of the case, summary of argument, argument with standard of review for each issue, conclusion, certificates. Word limits in FRAP 32(a)(7) (verify current figures). Check Eighth Circuit Local Rules and pro se guidance for addendum/appendix requirements and paper copies.
10. **Standards of review.** De novo (dismissal, summary judgment, legal questions); clear error (bench-trial facts); abuse of discretion (evidentiary rulings, discovery, leave to amend, preliminary injunctions — with legal conclusions de novo). Issues not raised below are generally forfeited. See `standard-of-review-finder`.
11. **After decision.** Petition for panel rehearing or rehearing en banc under FRAP 40 (en banc procedure was consolidated into Rule 40 by the December 2024 amendments; verify current time and length limits — typically 14 days after judgment, 45 when the United States is a party). Certiorari petition to the Supreme Court within 90 days after entry of the judgment or denial of a timely rehearing petition (Sup. Ct. R. 13).

## Pitfalls
- Filing a "motion for reconsideration" more than 28 days after judgment and assuming it tolls the appeal clock
- Appealing a non-final order without a 54(b) certification or statutory basis (dismissed for lack of jurisdiction)
- Filing the notice in the court of appeals instead of the district court
- Raising new arguments or evidence on appeal
- Failing to order transcripts, leaving no record to support the issues
- Missing the briefing deadline set by the clerk (dismissal for failure to prosecute)

## Verify before relying
- Pull FRAP 3, 4, 8, 10, 24, 28, 31, 32, 40, 28 U.S.C. 1291, 1292, and Eighth Circuit Local Rules verbatim via `statute-lookup` or Descrybe `search_laws_and_rules`; FRAP is amended most Decembers.
- Recompute every deadline from the docket entry date, applying FRAP 26 computation.
- Check that cases cited for appellate jurisdiction are still good law (CourtListener / Descrybe).
- Legal information, not legal advice; the Eighth Circuit and local law schools sometimes offer pro bono appellate programs.
